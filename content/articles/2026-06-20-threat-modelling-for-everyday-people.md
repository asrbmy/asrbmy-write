Security advice often arrives as a long checklist. A checklist is not a plan, because it does not know what matters to you. A **threat model** is the plan: a short, honest answer to "what could go wrong, and how much do I care?"

## Five questions

Write a sentence or two for each.

1. **What do I want to protect?** Photos, accounts, money, location, your identity?
2. **Who might want it?** A stranger, an advertiser, a former partner, an employer?
3. **How likely is it?** Be realistic about the common cases and the rare ones.
4. **How bad would it be if it happened?** Embarrassing, expensive, dangerous?
5. **How much effort am I willing to spend?** Protection that is too annoying will be abandoned.

## An example

Here is how the answers might look for an ordinary person with a few online accounts.

| Asset | Who might want it | Likelihood | Impact | Sensible response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Email account | Criminals running credential-stuffing attacks | High | High | Unique password, two-factor authentication |
| Browsing history | Ad networks | Very high | Low | Tracker blocking, separate profiles |
| Family photos | Cloud outages, lost phones | Medium | Medium | Regular backups in two places |
| Home address | Data brokers | Medium | Medium | Opt out where possible, share less |

## What the table shows

The biggest risks are boring. Protecting your email account — the key that resets everything else — matters far more than exotic tools. Spend effort where likelihood and impact are both high, and relax about the rest.

## Revisit it

Your situation changes: a new job, a new relationship, a public project. Review your answers once a year or whenever something significant changes.
